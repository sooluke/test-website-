Why Simple Ideas Are Often the Hardest to Build

Simple products often look obvious after they are finished. Getting there, however, can require removing dozens of unnecessary ideas and decisions.

When starting a new project, it is tempting to add features immediately. More options seem to create more value. In practice, every additional feature creates another decision, another interface and another thing that needs maintenance.

A useful constraint is to ask one question: what is the smallest version that solves the actual problem?

This approach does not mean making something incomplete. It means being deliberate about what deserves to exist.

The Value of Working With Constraints

Constraints can feel restrictive when we start a project. There is a strange assumption that unlimited freedom produces better results.

In reality, limitations can make creative decisions easier. A small number of tools, a short deadline or a simple technical stack can force us to focus on what actually matters.

For this project, the constraint is intentional: keep the website fast, understandable and easy to maintain.

What I Learned From Building in Public

Building something privately can feel comfortable. You can change direction, remove mistakes and experiment without anyone watching.

Building in public introduces a different dynamic. You have to explain decisions while they are still being made. Sometimes that feels uncomfortable, but the process creates an unexpected benefit: clarity.

When you explain an unfinished idea to another person, weak assumptions become much easier to notice.

BBB September 12, 2026

Documenting the Process Instead of the Result

Most project pages show the final result. They rarely show the dozens of small decisions that came before it.

I think the process is often more interesting than the final screenshot. What failed? What changed? Which assumptions turned out to be wrong?

This blog is an attempt to document some of those decisions rather than only presenting polished outcomes.
